How to Ask for a Raise — With Numbers, Not Nerves

A five-step, evidence-first method for raise requests and counter-offers, with scripts, timing tips and what to ask for when the budget says no.

By the 34456.com Editorial Team · Fact-checked against IRS and CRA publications ·

Why it is worth the discomfort

A raise compounds. Every future percentage increase, bonus and retirement match is calculated on your base. A $4,000 bump at age 30, carried forward with ordinary annual increases, is worth well over $150,000 across a career before investment growth. Not asking is the expensive option.

Step 1: Build your evidence file

List three to five results with numbers: revenue generated, costs cut, hours saved, customers retained, people trained. Managers approve raises they can justify upward; give them the justification.

Step 2: Find your market number

Check posted salary ranges for your role and location (pay-transparency laws now require them in many places), professional surveys and government wage data. Pick a target at the upper half of the range you can defend.

Step 3: Choose the moment

Best times: right after a visible win, during budget planning (often a quarter before the fiscal year), or when your responsibilities have clearly grown. Avoid days of layoffs or bad quarterly news.

Step 4: Make the ask

“Over the last year I [result 1], [result 2] and [result 3]. Based on that impact and market data for this role, I'm asking to move my salary from $34,456 to $38,500. Can we discuss what it would take to make that happen this cycle?”

Then stop talking. Silence is part of the negotiation.

Step 5: Handle “not right now”

Ask what specifically would justify the increase and when it will be reviewed — and get it in writing. Meanwhile negotiate what's cheaper for the company but valuable to you: title, an extra week of PTO, remote days, training budget, a dated review, or a one-time bonus.

Countering a job offer

Express enthusiasm first, then ask: “Is there flexibility on the base?” If they move, ask about a signing bonus too. Never accept on the spot — a day to review is standard.

Frequently asked questions

How much of a raise should I ask for?

Commonly 5–10% for an in-role raise and 10–20% when changing employers, anchored to market data.

Should I mention a competing offer?

Only if it is real and you would take it. Bluffing can backfire.
